\begin{abstract}
\noindent
A Khmer speech system that meets an English word such as \emph{taxi} must first write it in Khmer script, in a way
a Khmer reader would pronounce like the English word. We found no suitable curated training dataset or established
benchmark for this task, so we trained models on English--Khmer word pairs found in web text and asked how to tell
whether their spellings are any good. We built a small character-level model from scratch and adapted a pretrained byte-level model, and we
released both. The central lesson concerns measurement. Scoring a spelling by exact agreement with one reference
spelling proved misleading: it rejected spellings a Khmer reader accepted and rewarded references the reader
rejected. Blinded human review, in which spellings are judged acceptable, not acceptable or uncertain, changed which
conclusions we could draw. Under that review, cleaner and larger real data looked encouraging, whereas longer
training and adding English pronunciation information gave no clear improvement. Irregularly spelled English words
remain the main weakness, and acceptable spellings are often present among a model's alternatives without being
chosen first.
\end{abstract}

\section{Problem and motivation}
\label{sec:intro}

Khmer text often contains English words and names. A Khmer text-to-speech system reads only Khmer script, so a
front end must rewrite each English word in Khmer letters before the speech model sees it. The spelling has to make
a Khmer reader say something close to the \emph{English} pronunciation: \emph{london} becomes \km{ឡុនដុន}, read
roughly as ``lon-don''.

This task is not translation; the meaning of the word plays no part. Nor is it the conversion of \emph{romanised
Khmer} (Khmer words typed in Latin letters, such as \emph{sok sabay} for \km{សុខសប្បាយ}) back into Khmer script.
Here the input is a genuine English word and the target is a Khmer approximation of its English sound.

The target also differs from established loan spellings. Khmer writers usually spell \emph{coffee} \km{កាហ្វេ},
a form that does not follow the English vowel sounds. A pronunciation-based spelling such as
\km{ខូហ្វី} is closer to English. Both appear in our web data, and only the second follows the task's target.

More than one spelling can be right. Khmer has two series of consonant letters, inherent vowels, stacked
consonants and small marks that shift a consonant's series, and many English sounds have no exact equivalent.
English \emph{f}, for example, can reasonably be written \km{ហ្វ} or \km{ផ}. A useful system therefore needs to
produce \emph{an} acceptable spelling, not \emph{the} spelling.

The central engineering difficulty is supervision. We found no curated English--Khmer phonetic dictionary to learn
from and no established test set that lists the acceptable spellings of each word. We had to build training data from noisy
web text and, just as importantly, find a way of measuring quality that a Khmer reader would agree with.

\section{Building a transliterator with limited data}
\label{sec:data}

\subsection{Two kinds of model}
We tried two approaches with different costs (Table~\ref{tab:models}).

The first is a \emph{small character-level model}: a standard encoder--decoder Transformer of 2.6 million
parameters, trained from scratch to map English letters to Khmer letters. It runs in tens of milliseconds on a CPU
with nothing but NumPy.

The second adapts \emph{ByT5-small} \citep{xue-etal-2022-byt5}, a 300-million-parameter text-to-text model that
reads and writes raw UTF-8 bytes instead of a fixed vocabulary of word pieces. Working on bytes suits a task about
spelling, and ByT5 has been used for transliteration of romanised South Asian text \citep{kirov-etal-2024-context}.
We adapted it with \emph{LoRA} \citep{hu-etal-2022-lora}, which keeps the pretrained weights frozen and trains a
small set of additional low-rank matrices (here about six million parameters) in the attention and feed-forward
layers.

Byte-level adaptation has a pitfall that an engineer can easily mistake for a bug. Every Khmer character takes
three UTF-8 bytes, and two of them are almost free to predict. Our first adaptation, which adapted only the attention
layers for a short time, produced the same spelling (\km{ហ្វេន}) for \val{e06.top} of \val{h.items} evaluation
words while its training loss fell steadily: the model had learned to write plausible Khmer bytes without reading
the input. An audit of the training and decoding pipeline found no defect that explained the collapse. Adapting the
feed-forward layers as well and training longer produced the first working adapter, one that follows its input; we
did not isolate which of the two changes mattered.

\subsection{Where the paired examples come from}
Khmer news and encyclopaedia text sometimes writes a foreign word in Khmer script followed by the original in
brackets. Searching the Khmer part of the FineWeb-2 web corpus \citep{penedo-etal-2025-fineweb2} and Khmer
Wikipedia for this pattern yields paired examples such as
\km{ហ្វេសប៊ុក}~(Facebook), shown in context in Figure~\ref{fig:pipeline}. We kept a pair only if the English word has an entry in the
CMU Pronouncing Dictionary \citep{cmudict}, so that its intended pronunciation is known, and passed it through
the checks described below. A further set of names and loanwords was taken from human translations in the ALT
Parallel Corpus \citep{riza-etal-2016-alt}, where the Khmer spelling occurs verbatim. None of these spellings was
reviewed by a person before training.

\paragraph{A filtering mistake that changed what the model could learn.}
The miner was also meant to discard romanised Khmer that happens to sit in brackets. Its rule dropped a pair if the
Latin side was a known romanised Khmer word \emph{or} if the Khmer side was a known Khmer word. The second
condition quietly removed every pair whose Khmer spelling appears in a Khmer dictionary, which includes well-known
loans such as \km{ហ្វេសប៊ុក}. As a result, none of the small model's training targets could be found in the
dictionary, a sign that revealed the error once noticed. Splitting the two conditions returned
\val{d.recovered} candidate pairs to the pool. The general lesson is to inspect what a filter actually removes,
not only how much.

\paragraph{What automatic checks can and cannot verify.}
To discard unrelated matches, a rule-based reader converts the Khmer consonants into sound classes and compares
them with the consonants of the English pronunciation. On held-out dictionary words it agreed with the
dictionary's own transcriptions for about \val{d.consrate}\% of words, so it is a useful consonant check. It does not
read vowels at all, and vowel differences turned out to be the largest single kind of disagreement between model
outputs and references (\val{vow.min}--\val{vow.max}\% of mismatches). Some valid pairs failed the consonant check
because of doubled letters or glides; a later pass recovered \val{d.r.recovered} of them, which also admits some
noise (the recovered pairs include a truncated \emph{hotel} $\rightarrow$ \km{តែល}).

\paragraph{Keeping evaluation words separate.}
English words come in families such as \emph{share}, \emph{shares}, \emph{sharing} and \emph{shared}, and a model that
has seen \emph{shared} has nearly seen \emph{share}. We therefore grouped words into families with a simple
suffix-stripping rule (removing endings such as \emph{-s}, \emph{-ed} and \emph{-ing}, and joining words that share
a stem) and kept every family on one side of each split. This is a heuristic, not a full morphological analysis:
irregular forms such as \emph{ran} and \emph{run} are not grouped. Evaluation words were removed from all training
files by family before training.

The released adapter was trained on \val{d.r.rows} pairs: screened web pairs, recovered pairs and ALT-attested
spellings. In one earlier experiment we also added \val{d.synth.rows} spellings suggested by Google's AI Mode; they made no measurable difference and are not used by either released model.

\section{Measuring acceptable transliteration}
\label{sec:eval}

Our first metric was the usual one: how often the model's first answer exactly matched a reference spelling, with
references taken from the mined data. For a task with several acceptable answers and noisy references, this metric
fails in two directions.

It can reject an acceptable spelling. For the development word \emph{band}, the released adapter wrote \km{បេន},
which a Khmer reader pronounces close to the English word. The mined reference was \km{បាន}, so the metric scored
the adapter as wrong. It can also reward an unacceptable one. Earlier models wrote \km{បាន} for \emph{band}; it
matched the reference and scored as correct, yet \km{បាន} is also an everyday Khmer word read roughly ``baan'', and
the reviewer judged it not acceptable. The same pattern appeared for \emph{phi}: the released adapter's \km{ភី}
matched the reference and was rejected, while an earlier model's \km{ហ្វី} missed it and was accepted.

These were not isolated cases. In the development review described below, \val{x.diff.acc} of the \val{x.diff}
spellings that differed from the mined reference were acceptable, and \val{x.match.not} of the \val{x.match} that
matched it were not. In a separate diagnostic on hard words, the reviewer accepted two or three different candidate
spellings for \val{cov.multi} of \val{cov.words} words. Exact agreement with one reference therefore measures resemblance to a
noisy label, not phonetic acceptability. Shared tasks with reference \emph{lists} \citep{chen-etal-2018-news}
address the first failure but not the second.

\paragraph{Blinded review.}
We replaced single-reference scoring with human judgments under a written policy fixed before any review. For each
word the reviewer saw the English word and its intended reading in the dictionary's pronunciation symbols (for
example \texttt{B AE1 N D} for \emph{band}). Below it were the candidate spellings from the compared models, with model identities,
ranks and scores hidden, in random order and with duplicates shown once. Each spelling received one of three
labels: \emph{acceptable} if a Khmer reader would pronounce it close to the intended reading, \emph{not
acceptable}, or \emph{uncertain}. The reviewer could accept several spellings or none and never had to type a
spelling. ``Acceptable'' in our results means confirmed acceptable; uncertain answers stay uncertain and count as
not confirmed.

\paragraph{Development review and prospective tests.}
Two kinds of review must be kept apart. The \emph{development review} looked back at existing models on 60
development words, the set also used to select checkpoints: 40 sampled to be representative and 20 chosen because
the models disagreed. Its answers were
given in free text and mapped to the three labels under rules the reviewer confirmed, for example that a candidate
not named in the reply is not acceptable. It guided decisions but is exploratory. The two \emph{prospective tests}
each used 120 fresh words (40 ordinary words, 40 irregularly spelled words and 40 names), chosen and frozen
before the candidate model was trained, with all outputs frozen before review. Each spelling was judged with an
explicit click on a review page. The two test samples are different, so their rates are not comparable with each
other.

All judgments come from one reviewer, the project's author, who is a Khmer reader but not independent of the
project. The results are therefore task-specific evidence for one reader and one policy. The 40/40/40 design
deliberately over-represents hard words and names, so the rates do not estimate performance on everyday text.

\subsection{More real data: encouraging, if measured well}
\emph{Why we tried it.} The filtering mistake and the consonant check had discarded many genuine pairs. Recovering
them and adding corpus-attested spellings roughly doubled the adapter's data.

\emph{What happened.} Reference agreement barely moved: the larger-data adapter and the first working adapter
matched the mined references about equally often. The development review told a different story. The larger-data
model's spelling was acceptable more often in both the representative and the disagreement-enriched samples, which
are reported separately because they were drawn differently. A version that also used the AI-suggested spellings
performed like the real-data version, so the gain did not depend on them.

\emph{What it means.} The data change looked useful only once we measured acceptability, so the strength of this
conclusion rests on the evaluation. Because the review was post hoc and had one reviewer, we treat it as an
encouraging development result rather than an established improvement, and we released the real-data model.

\subsection{Longer training: no demonstrated benefit}
\emph{Why we tried it.} The selected checkpoint of the released adapter was the last one evaluated, which raised the
possibility that it was under-trained.

\emph{What happened.} Doubling the number of training updates changed \val{t1.changed} of the 120 test spellings
but did not change the overall acceptance rate: both models were acceptable for \val{t1.e11.acceptable} of 120
words, each winning on 14 words the other lost. The sample was too small to rule out modest differences in either
direction.

\emph{What it means.} More training rearranged the errors without reducing them. With noisy, incomplete
supervision, training longer is not a reliable fix; the model may simply fit the noise differently.

\subsection{Pronunciation alongside letters: no clear benefit}
\label{sec:res-pron}
\emph{Why we tried it.} English spelling is a poor guide to English sound, and earlier transliteration work gained
from combining letters with pronunciation \citep{oh-choi-2005-ensemble,ngo-etal-2015-phonology}. Supplying the
dictionary pronunciation seemed likely to help with words like \emph{colonel}.

\emph{What happened.} For the small model, we gave the pronunciation symbols as a second input next to the letters.
Across three comparisons on different data the differences were small and uncertain; the registered comparison is
in Appendix~\ref{app:methods}. A diagnostic explains part of this. When we swapped in another word's pronunciation,
the model's first answer changed for \val{sens.min}--\val{sens.max}\% of words, depending on the training run, but
the changed answers rarely moved towards the other word's consonants; most changes were vowel or diacritic edits.

For the adapted model, we appended the pronunciation to the input. A \emph{word-only} input such as
\texttt{fire} became the \emph{pronunciation-guided} input \texttt{fire|F AY1 ER0}, where the symbols spell the
English sounds and the digits mark stress (1 for primary stress, 0 for none). No audio is involved, and the output
is still Khmer text. This model also received the reviewer's accepted spellings for 42 difficult training word families and
started from the released adapter. On the second test sample it was acceptable for exactly as many words as the
released adapter. Removing the pronunciation changed only about \val{e13.mech.missing}\% of its development outputs,
so the model still relied mainly on the letters.

\emph{What it means.} In these models and data, extra pronunciation input was largely ignored. This does not show
that pronunciation cannot help Khmer transliteration, and because the second test changed several ingredients
together, it cannot isolate the effect of pronunciation. A model may need training data in which spelling and sound
disagree often enough to force it to read the pronunciation.

\subsection{A decoding setting, not the model, caused a beam-search deficit}
\label{sec:res-decode}
\emph{Why we looked.} For the adapted models, beam search, which keeps several partial spellings and returns the
most probable complete one, scored worse than simply taking the most likely byte at each step (greedy decoding).
That is unusual.

\emph{What happened.} The beam search had been called with early stopping enabled. At the byte level, junk
hypotheses that end after one or two bytes finish first and fill the set of completed answers before real spellings
end. The search then returned truncated or less probable spellings: for the development word \emph{mani}, greedy
decoding gave \km{ម៉ានី} but the beam returned \km{មានី}, dropping the mark that sets the consonant's series. With
early stopping disabled, beam search matched greedy decoding and clearly beat the original beam setting
(Table~\ref{tab:comparisons} in Appendix~\ref{app:methods}).

\emph{What it means.} Check generation settings before attributing a difference to model quality. Earlier beam-search
comparisons in this project were shifted by the defect, so we report greedy results for the adapted models.

\subsection{Acceptable spellings are often generated but not chosen}
\label{sec:res-rank}
\emph{Why we looked.} Irregularly spelled words were the weakest group in both tests: the released adapter was
acceptable for \val{t1.irregular.e11} and \val{t2.irregular.e11} of 40 such words. We wanted to know whether the
model could not produce an acceptable spelling at all, or produced one without ranking it first.

\emph{What happened.} For \val{cov.words} irregular development words, the reviewer judged every distinct spelling
among the model's greedy answer and its four beam-search candidates (Figure~\ref{fig:coverage}). The greedy answer was
acceptable for \val{cov.greedy} words and the top beam candidate for \val{cov.beam1}, but at least one of the four
candidates was acceptable for \val{cov.any4}. For \val{cov.none} words no candidate was acceptable.

built, and it cannot tell us whether selection or generation matters more on ordinary text.

\section{Practical lessons and limitations}
\label{sec:lessons}

Several lessons should transfer to other low-resource transliteration projects.
\begin{itemize}
\item \emph{Audit what filtering rules remove.} Our miner silently discarded every pair whose Khmer side was a
  dictionary word. Looking at examples of dropped data, not just counts, would have caught it at once.
\item \emph{Check the generation configuration before blaming the model.} A single decoding flag produced an
  apparent beam-search deficit that looked like a modelling problem.
\item \emph{Do not confuse agreement with one reference with acceptability.} When several spellings are valid and
  references are noisy, exact matching rejects good outputs and rewards bad references. Blinded judgments with
  explicit ``uncertain'' answers were more informative than any automatic score we had.
\item \emph{More training does not repair conflicting or incomplete supervision.} A longer schedule changed many
  outputs without improving them.
\item \emph{Treat candidate coverage as an opportunity, not a result.} Acceptable alternatives in a beam show that
  a selector could help; they are not the accuracy of one.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Limitations.}
Irregular English spellings remain the main weakness: on the two tests, the released adapter was acceptable for
\val{irr.lo}--\val{irr.hi}\% of irregular words. The training data are mined and unreviewed, and the automatic
checks do not cover vowels. All human judgments come from one project-affiliated reviewer on samples of 120 words,
so differences of several percentage points cannot be resolved, and each adapter was trained once. Much of the
reference-based evidence uses one historical evaluation set that was examined across many experiments. The two
released models were trained on different data and were never evaluated on a common, untouched test, so we do not
compare them with each other. Only single words are handled; sentence context is out of scope.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

Spelling English words in Khmer script is a small task with an awkward property: many answers are right, and we
found no reliable list of them. With only noisy web pairs for supervision, the most consequential change in this
project was not a model but a measurement: replacing exact agreement with one reference by blinded judgments of
acceptability. Under that measurement, cleaner and larger real data looked encouraging, while longer training and
added pronunciation information showed no clear benefit. The released adapter produces an acceptable spelling for
\val{t1.e11.share}\% and \val{t2.e11.share}\% of words in two separate, deliberately hard 120-word samples,
and for about half or fewer of the irregularly spelled ones. Acceptable alternatives are often among its candidates, which points to candidate
selection and reviewed data as the next steps.

\section{Methods in brief}
\label{app:methods}

\paragraph{Models and training.} \emph{Small model:} encoder--decoder Transformer, model width 192, three encoder
and three decoder layers, four attention heads, feed-forward width 512; AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$ with cosine
decay, label smoothing 0.1, 40 epochs with the epoch chosen on \val{s.dev.n} development items; beam search of width
4. \emph{Adapted model:} ByT5-small frozen; LoRA rank 16, $\alpha=32$, on all attention projections and
feed-forward layers (\val{x.params} trained parameters); AdamW, learning rate $10^{-3}$, 100 warm-up steps then
linear decay, batch 32, \val{e11.update} updates on a laptop CPU, one training run; checkpoint chosen by exact
agreement on \val{d.items} development items, then character error rate. Single answers use greedy decoding;
candidate lists use beam search with early stopping disabled. Full configurations are in the repository's
experiment folders and model cards.

\paragraph{Data.} The adapter's \val{d.r.rows} pairs (\val{d.r.words} words) are \val{d.r.control} screened web
pairs, \val{d.r.recovered} pairs recovered from the consonant check and \val{d.r.alt} ALT-attested pairs. The small
model used \val{s.train} web pairs mined before the filtering repair.
